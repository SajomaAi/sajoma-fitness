# YouTube Channel Security & Growth Playbook

This skill captures the channel-security and channel-growth strategy the user follows for their YouTube channels (Real Estate with Basilia, BW Auto Repair, Sajoma Fitness). When advising on anything YouTube-related, apply these rules and recommendations consistently.

## Part 1 — Channel Security

### The core rule: the Google account owner IS the channel owner

- Whoever controls the Google account legally owns the channel and all its content, and can delete the entire channel. Many large creators lost everything by giving agencies or employees full account access — in one case an agency deleted a massive Hispanic creator's whole channel when the relationship ended.
- **Never let an agency or third party create the channel/account for you.** Always create the Google account yourself. If a third party already holds ownership, you may need to negotiate to regain control.

### 1. Use a dedicated "secret" (blinded) email

- Create the channel's Google account with an email **used for nothing else** — no personal correspondence, no social media, no public-facing activity.
- Keep the address completely private, **even from your own team**. Nobody should know it exists or where it is.
- Immediately protect it with physical 2FA and backup recovery options.

### 2. Physical two-factor authentication for everyone

- You **and every team member with any level of access** must use 2FA — preferably a physical authenticator app, not just SMS.
- Maintain backup accounts and backup phone numbers so access is never lost.
- Verify a person has 2FA enabled **before** granting them any channel access.

### 3. Never share account credentials

- Never give the Google account password or direct login to anyone — not consultants, not agencies, not key employees. Direct access lets them delete the account or change ownership.
- All team access goes through YouTube Studio permission levels instead.

### 4. YouTube Studio permission levels

| Role | Can delete channel | Sees revenue/AdSense | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Owner** | **Yes (only role that can)** | Yes — manages AdSense, monetization, brand deals, where money is sent | Should only ever be the user |
| **Administrator** | No | Yes (view revenue) | Manages channel, live streams; high-level but cannot delete |
| **Editor** | No | Yes (monetization/analytics data) | Manages and schedules content; the role consultants typically request |
| **Limited Editor** | No | **No — blinded to all financial data** | Manages/schedules content; ideal for staff who shouldn't see earnings |

### 5. Minimum Access Principle

- Always grant the **lowest permission level needed** for the job. A consultant reviewing analytics gets Editor (or Limited Editor if revenue should stay private) — never Administrator or Owner. Reputable consultants themselves say: "Don't give me owner, don't give me administrator — give me the minimum I need."
- Staff who only upload/schedule videos → **Limited Editor**, so revenue stays private.
- This protects against disgruntled employees posting inappropriate content that could get the channel permanently banned.
- Periodically audit the access list in YouTube Studio: confirm the user is the Owner, review every person's role, and downgrade anyone with more access than their job requires.

## Part 2 — Channel Growth Strategy

### Define the Avatar (ideal viewer)

- Content is for a **specific person with a specific problem** your unique talent can solve — not "everyone." (E.g., a 40-year-old woman recovering her fitness, not "all women.")
- Study their needs and fears better than they do: read comments, and use Ask Studio (Gemini) to summarize what the audience asks for most.
- A defined avatar prevents "vanity metrics" — big view counts from the wrong audience that don't build a business and confuse the algorithm's AI tools.

### Stay tightly niched

- The algorithm behaves like a person: if the channel mixes unrelated topics ("chile, mole, and manteca"), it can't tell who to recommend videos to and stops recommending.
- Tight niching is also what activates YouTube's free AI tools — they only work with "clean data" from a clear niche.

### Use YouTube's built-in AI tools (free)

- **Inspiration Tab**: generates intros, hooks, logical video structure, competitive analysis, and niche demand data. Works for ~90% of educational channels (rarely for broad podcasts). Primarily in English — run suggestions through Google Translate for other languages. If it's missing or says "insufficient data," the niche isn't clear enough yet.
- **Ask Studio (Gemini)**: summarizes comment-section data (frequent questions, fears, doubts), helps with thumbnails and intros. Requires sufficient data and a clear niche.

### Content strategy

- **Search-driven content**: answer the specific questions the avatar is actually searching for.
- **Match the viewer's mode**: short punchy videos for quick phone searches; extra-long 1.5–2 hour masterclasses for viewers ready to sit down and learn.
- **Think in series (Netflix-style binge)**: never launch with one video — start with at least two playlists of 2–3 linked videos each. Use end screens to route viewers to the next video; otherwise YouTube recommends competitors. Goal: 3–7 videos watched per session.
- **Visual promise**: banner must state who you are, what the channel is about, and "why you?" Set a trailer for non-subscribers and a featured video for subscribers in Studio's Personalization tab. Use a 20–30 second welcome video stating the problem you solve and the channel's promise.
- **Intro confirms the thumbnail's promise**: aim for 75–80% retention in the first minute. If retention drops in the first 30 seconds, study why and fix the next video.

### Posting frequency and timing

- **First 3–6 months are a learning sprint**: frequency beats extreme quality. Post often (aim for at least 1–2 videos per week; more is better early on) to find your voice, practice in public, and generate data. If a video fails, change the thumbnail or intro and retry.
- **Consistency beats any "magic" day or time.** Pick a schedule you can sustain and keep it, so the audience and algorithm learn when to expect you.
- **Let your own data pick the time**: once there is data, use YouTube Studio → Analytics → Audience → "When your viewers are on YouTube," and publish 1–2 hours **before** the peak activity window so the video has ratings/velocity when your audience arrives.
- Sensible starting defaults until data exists (general best practice, then adjust to your Studio data): weekdays mid-afternoon (2–4 pm in the audience's timezone, catching after-work/evening viewing) and weekend mornings (9–11 am). For a Spanish-speaking / US-Hispanic audience, consider evening hours (6–9 pm local).
- **Iterate on data, not perfection**: review retention and click-through after each video and adjust topic, thumbnail, intro, and posting slot accordingly.

## How to apply this skill

- Security questions (access, roles, agencies, passwords, hacks) → answer from Part 1; always reinforce: never share credentials, minimum access, secret email, physical 2FA.
- Growth questions (what/when to post, thumbnails, intros, analytics, AI tools) → answer from Part 2; always anchor advice to the avatar, the niche, and the user's own YouTube Studio data.
- When setting up anything new (channel, team member, consultant engagement), walk through the relevant checklist above step by step.
